# HINSPHERES workflow

The hinspheres package implements a forward-modelling framework for the HI 21-cm line radiative transfer through spherically symmetric molecular cloud cores, coupled to a CMA-ES optimisation engine that recovers the underlying physical parameters from observed HINSA data cubes. The cloud is discretised into $N$ concentric spherical shells of equal thickness $\Delta r = R_\mathrm{out} / N$, where $R_\mathrm{out}$ is the total cloud radius. Each shell $k$ ($k = 1 \dots N$) has inner radius $r_{\mathrm{inner},k} = (k-1)\Delta r$, outer radius $r_{\mathrm{outer},k} = k\Delta r$, and midpoint radius $r_{\mathrm{mid},k} = (r_{\mathrm{inner},k} + r_{\mathrm{outer},k})/2$, and is assigned uniform physical properties: the total hydrogen number density $n_\mathrm{H}(r_k)$, the HI spin temperature $T_s(r_k)$, the HI abundance $f_\mathrm{HI}(r_k) = n_\mathrm{HI} / n_\mathrm{H}$, and the line-of-sight velocity $v_\mathrm{los}(r_k)$. The radial density profile follows a Plummer law, $n_\mathrm{H}(r) = \rho_0 / \bigl[1 + (r/r_0)^\alpha\bigr]$, where $\rho_0$ is the central H-nucleus density, $r_0$ the Plummer core radius, and $\alpha$ the power-law index. The spin temperature adopts a Plummer-like form, $T_s(r) = T_1 + (T_0 - T_1) / \bigl[1 + (r/r_T)^2\bigr]$, with $T_0$ the central cold-core temperature, $T_1$ the ambient envelope temperature, and $r_T$ the temperature transition radius; temperatures are floored at $T_\mathrm{CMB} = 2.725$ K. The HI abundance can be specified either directly as a per-shell array $\{f_{\mathrm{HI},k}\}$ or parametrically via a unimodal profile in which $f_\mathrm{HI}$ peaks at a designated shell index $k_\mathrm{peak}$ (the peak shell) and decreases monotonically outward through geometric multipliers, $f_{\mathrm{HI},k} = f_{\mathrm{HI},k-1} \cdot m_k$, where each $m_k \in (0, 1]$. The kinematics of the cloud are modelled as a superposition of spherical infall and rigid-body rotation. The infall speed at radius $r$ is a fixed fraction of the free-fall velocity, $v_\mathrm{inf}(r) = f_\mathrm{ff} \sqrt{2 G M(<r) / r}$, where $M(<r)$ is the enclosed mass integrated from the density profile and $f_\mathrm{ff}$ is a dimensionless free-fall fraction between 0 and 1. The projected line-of-sight component of the infall velocity at a point with impact parameter $b$ and depth $z$ along the line of sight is $v_\mathrm{los}^\mathrm{inf} = -v_\mathrm{inf}(r) \cdot \mathrm{sign}(z) \cdot z/r$, where $r = \sqrt{b^2 + z^2}$. Rigid-body rotation contributes $v_\mathrm{los}^\mathrm{rot} = \Omega (-\sin PA \cdot dy - \cos PA \cdot dx)$, where $\Omega = v_\mathrm{rot} / R_\mathrm{out}$ is the angular speed, $PA$ is the position angle of the rotation axis measured east of north, and $(dx, dy)$ are the sky-plane offsets from the cloud centre in the FITS west-north convention. The total line-of-sight velocity entering the radiative transfer is then $v_\mathrm{los}(r, z) = v_\mathrm{los}^\mathrm{inf}(r, z) + v_\mathrm{los}^\mathrm{rot}(dx, dy) + V_\mathrm{LSR} + v_\mathrm{offset}$, where $V_\mathrm{LSR}$ is the systemic velocity of the cloud and $v_\mathrm{offset}$ is an optional empirical shift.

For each spatial pixel of the output data cube, the code computes the impact parameter $b = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$. If $b \ge R_\mathrm{out}$, the pixel lies outside the cloud and the spectrum is left unchanged. Otherwise, the line of sight is traced through the spherical shell stack to obtain an ordered list of layers, each corresponding to a single shell intersection with a known path length $\Delta l_k$ and depth coordinate $z_k$. The front and back hemispheres are traversed separately: the ray enters the outermost shell on the far side ($z < 0$), proceeds inward to the shell containing $b$, then exits through the near side ($z > 0$) towards the observer. Each layer is characterised by its peak line-centre optical depth,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{0,k} = \frac{3 c^2 A_{10}}{8\pi \nu_{21}^2} \frac{n_{\mathrm{HI},k} \, \Delta l_k}{T_{s,k} \, \sigma_k \sqrt{2\pi}},
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the speed of light, $A_{10} = 2.884\times10^{-15}$ s$^{-1}$ the Einstein spontaneous emission coefficient for the 21-cm hyperfine transition, $\nu_{21} = 1.420405751$ GHz the transition frequency, $n_{\mathrm{HI},k}$ the atomic hydrogen number density in the shell, $\Delta l_k$ the path length through the shell, $T_{s,k}$ the spin temperature, and $\sigma_k$ the velocity dispersion given by $\sigma_k = \sqrt{k_B T_{s,k} / \mu m_\mathrm{H} + \sigma_\mathrm{turb}^2}$, where $\sigma_\mathrm{turb}$ is a constant turbulent velocity width supplied by the user. The optical depth at any velocity offset $v$ from the line centre follows a Gaussian profile,
\begin{equation}
\tau_k(v) = \tau_{0,k} \, \exp\!\left[-\frac{(v - v_{\mathrm{center},k})^2}{2 \sigma_k^2}\right],
\end{equation}
with $v_{\mathrm{center},k}$ the total line-of-sight velocity of that layer.

The radiative transfer along a single line of sight is governed by the Li & Goldsmith (2003) three-component model. In this formalism, the observed brightness temperature $T_B(v)$ is the result of background HI emission (including both the Galactic foreground and the true background behind the cloud) passing through the cold cloud, which is treated as a stack of $N$ homogeneous layers, followed by the foreground Galactic HI in front of the cloud. The order of propagation is: background HI $\rightarrow$ cold cloud (shells in order of increasing distance from the observer) $\rightarrow$ foreground HI $\rightarrow$ observer. The full transfer equation reads
\begin{equation}
T_B(v) = T_\mathrm{bg}(v) \exp\!\Bigl[-\sum_{k=1}^N \tau_k(v)\Bigr] \exp[-\tau_\mathrm{fg}(v)] + \sum_{k=1}^N T_{s,k} \bigl[1 - e^{-\tau_k(v)}\bigr] \exp\!\Bigl[-\sum_{j=k+1}^N \tau_j(v)\Bigr] \exp[-\tau_\mathrm{fg}(v)] + T_\mathrm{fg} \bigl[1 - e^{-\tau_\mathrm{fg}(v)}\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $T_\mathrm{bg}(v)$ is the brightness temperature of the background HI incident on the far side of the cloud, $T_\mathrm{fg}$ and $\tau_\mathrm{fg}$ are the spin temperature and the total optical depth of the foreground Galactic HI layer. In practice, the foreground HI optical depth is computed from a Gaussian model of the Galactic HI vertical distribution (Lockman 1984; Li & Goldsmith 2003), $\tau_\mathrm{fg} = (1-p) \tau_{h,\mathrm{total}}$, where the covering fraction $p = \mathrm{erfc}\bigl[\sqrt{4\ln 2}\, D \sin|b| / z_0\bigr]$ depends on the cloud distance $D$, its Galactic latitude $b$, and the HI disk FWHM $z_0 = 360$ pc. The code first strips the foreground from the observed background cube before applying the cloud RT, then re-applies the foreground to the synthetic output so that the resulting cube has the same observational character as the input data. When the foreground contribution is negligible (as is the case for high-latitude clouds), the user may disable this step entirely by setting $\tau_{h,\mathrm{total}} = 0$.

For forward generation, the `build_synthetic_hinsa` function orchestrates the computation across all pixels. It begins by evaluating the radial profiles from the input parameter set, constructing the impact-parameter map for the entire spatial grid, and then dispatching the per-pixel ray-tracing and RT calculation in parallel via `joblib.Parallel`. At each pixel, the code calls `los_path_lengths` to obtain the ordered layer list, computes $\tau_{0,k}$, $v_{\mathrm{center},k}$, and $\sigma_k$ for each layer, and passes these arrays together with the local background spectrum (extracted from the background cube at that pixel) to `radiative_transfer_pixel`, which evaluates equation (3) for every velocity channel. The background cube supplied to the forward model is the observed HI brightness temperature cube *without* the cold cloud — that is, it already contains the sum of Galactic foreground and distant background HI emission. The foreground is stripped, the cloud RT is applied to the pure background, and the foreground is restored, as described above. The result is a synthetic data cube $T_B(v, x, y)$ that can be directly compared with an observed HINSA cube. A high-level wrapper `generate_sim_hinsa` provides a convenient interface that loads a FITS background cube (or creates a constant-temperature background), handles the velocity-axis flipping, applies optional spatial and velocity Gaussian smoothing to match telescope resolution, and writes the output FITS file along with a diagnostic PNG and a JSON metadata file containing all configuration and parameters.

The inverse problem — recovering physical parameters from an observed HINSA cube — is solved by minimising a weighted residual between the observed and forward-modelled cubes. The residual for a single velocity channel is defined as
\begin{equation}
R = \frac{\sum_{j,i} w(j,i) \; \bigl[T_\mathrm{obs}(v, j,i) - T_\mathrm{model}(v, j,i)\bigr]^2 \; / \; |T_\mathrm{obs}(v, j,i)|}{\sum_{j,i} w(j,i)},
\end{equation}
where $(j,i)$ runs over all spatial pixels and $w(j,i)$ is a radial weight designed to compensate for the geometric bias inherent in a spherical model. Because the number of pixels within an annulus at radius $r$ scales as $2\pi r \, dr$, a uniform weight would cause the outer rings to dominate the residual and drive the fit toward fitting the envelope at the expense of the core. The weight $w(j,i) = 1 / r(j,i)$ cancels this area effect so that each equidistant ring contributes equally to the total residual; the centre pixel ($r=0$) is assigned the weight of its nearest neighbours, $w = 1 / \Delta r_\mathrm{pix}$, to avoid division by zero. The normalisation by $\sum w$ renders the residual independent of the map size and yields a value in kelvins that is zero for a perfect fit.

The minimisation is performed by the CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy) algorithm in a normalised parameter space. All $M$ free physical parameters are mapped linearly from their physical bounds $[\theta_\mathrm{lo}, \theta_\mathrm{hi}]$ to the unit hypercube $[0, 1]^M$ via $x_m = (\theta_m - \theta_{\mathrm{lo},m}) / (\theta_{\mathrm{hi},m} - \theta_{\mathrm{lo},m})$. The CMA-ES starts from an initial mean vector $\mathbf{x}_0$ (corresponding to the user-supplied initial guess or the package defaults) and an initial step size $\sigma_0 = 0.2$, and at each generation it samples a population of candidate vectors from a multivariate Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{\mu}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{C})$ using the `ask` method, evaluates the residual for each candidate by mapping back to physical parameters, calling `build_synthetic_hinsa`, and computing equation (4), then updates the mean and covariance with the `tell` method based on the ranked residuals. The algorithm terminates when the step size falls below a tolerance, the function value stagnates, or the maximum number of generations is reached. Upon convergence, the parameter mean $\mathbf{\mu}$ gives the best-fit values, and the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C}$ provides a estimate of the $1\sigma$ uncertainties under the assumption that the residual landscape is locally Gaussian: the standard deviation of the $m$-th parameter in physical space is $\sigma_{\theta,m} \approx \sigma_{x,m} \cdot (\theta_{\mathrm{hi},m} - \theta_{\mathrm{lo},m})$, where $\sigma_{x,m}$ is the square root of the $m$-th diagonal element of $\mathbf{C}$.

The package also offers a `second_derivative` fitting mode that does not require a separate background FITS cube. In this mode, the candidate physical model is used to compute the layer optical depths via equation (1), and an inverse radiative transfer routine peels back the cloud layers from the observed spectrum to reconstruct the unabsorbed background, $T_\mathrm{bg}(v)$. The inversion proceeds layer by layer from near to far, applying
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{before},k}(v) = \frac{T_{\mathrm{after},k}(v) - T_{s,k}\bigl[1 - e^{-\tau_k(v)}\bigr]}{e^{-\tau_k(v)}}
\end{equation}
for each shell, with a numerical cap on $e^{-\tau_k(v)}$ at 0.01 (corresponding to $\tau_k \lesssim 4.6$) to prevent divergent amplification of model-data mismatch in optically thick channels. The residual in this mode is the integrated squared second derivative of the reconstructed background,
\begin{equation}
R = \frac{\sum_{j,i,w} w(j,i) \; \bigl[d^2 T_\mathrm{bg}(v, j,i) / dv^2\bigr]^2 \; \Delta v}{\sum_{j,i,w} w(j,i)},
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all pixels and all velocity channels. The rationale is that a physically correct model should strip the absorption features cleanly, leaving a spectrally smooth background and therefore a small second derivative. This mode has the practical advantage of bypassing the need for an independently extracted background cube, but it suffers from a known degeneracy: as the cloud optical depth approaches zero ($\tau_k \rightarrow 0$), the inverse RT leaves the observed spectrum essentially unchanged, yielding $T_\mathrm{bg}(v) \approx T_\mathrm{obs}(v)$ and a near-zero R-value regardless of the model parameters. The CMA-ES therefore tends to converge to this trivial solution (low density, low abundance) unless constrained. For this reason the forward mode is recommended for production fitting, and the second-derivative mode should be regarded as a diagnostic or as a fallback when no reliable background cube is available.

The full fitting workflow is exposed through two entry points. The low-level function `fit_hinspheres` accepts pre-loaded numpy arrays and a `Config` object, performs the CMA-ES optimisation, and returns the best-fit parameter dictionary together with the optimiser result and the per-parameter $1\sigma$ uncertainties. The high-level wrapper `fit_hinsa_model` accepts FITS file paths, reads the data and headers automatically, computes the pixel scale and velocity axis from the FITS metadata, extracts the Galactic latitude from the WCS coordinates, optionally performs a scan over all integer values of `peak_shell` to break the degeneracy between the abundance peak location and the multiplier scale, runs the CMA-ES at each peak shell value, selects the run with the lowest residual, applies spatial and velocity smoothing if requested, and saves the best-fit model cube, a diagnostic 6-panel figure (radial profiles, moment-zero map, peak absorption map, central spectrum), a grid-spectra figure showing the observed and model spectra at a regular grid of positions, and a comprehensive NPZ archive containing the best-fit parameters, uncertainties, residual, model cube, observed cube, background cube, velocity axis, and metadata.
